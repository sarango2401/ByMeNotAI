In most introductory physics courses, entropy is defined as *disorder*. Ice melting, smoke spreading, a glass shattering — all of these increase entropy, we are told, because the system moves from ordered to disordered.

This is not wrong, but it is not quite right either. And the gap between the two is where things get interesting.

## A better definition

The statistical mechanician Ludwig Boltzmann gave us a cleaner way to think about entropy: it counts the number of microscopic arrangements of a system that are consistent with its macroscopic appearance.

A glass of water has an enormous number of possible arrangements of its molecules — roughly the same temperature and volume, but molecules shuffling constantly. A perfectly ordered ice crystal has far fewer. Entropy is high when there are many ways to be the same thing.

The reason entropy tends to increase is therefore almost embarrassingly simple: there are more disordered states than ordered ones. Random change is statistically overwhelmingly likely to move a system toward one of the many disordered configurations, not one of the few ordered ones.

## What this has to do with time

Here is the strange part. Every fundamental law of physics — Newton's laws, electromagnetism, quantum mechanics — is reversible in time. Run any of them backwards, and the equations still hold.

Yet entropy only increases forward in time. A coffee cup does not spontaneously reassemble from shards. Heat does not flow from cold objects to hot ones.

This asymmetry is called the *arrow of time*, and its origin remains one of the deepest open questions in physics. Our best current answer is that the universe began in an extraordinarily low-entropy state — the Big Bang — and has been spreading out ever since.
