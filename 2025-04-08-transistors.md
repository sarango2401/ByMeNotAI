In 1947, three physicists at Bell Labs built a device from germanium, gold foil, and a paperclip that would eventually become the most manufactured object in human history. The transistor is now made at densities of over a hundred million per square millimeter — smaller than a wavelength of visible light.

This is the story of how that happened.

## What a transistor does

At its core, a transistor is a switch controlled by electricity. Apply a small voltage to one terminal (the gate), and current flows between the other two (the source and drain). Remove it, and the current stops.

That is genuinely all it does. The power comes from doing it billions of times per second, in a space measured in atoms.

## Why silicon won

The first transistors were made of germanium, which was easier to purify. But silicon has a critical advantage: when it oxidizes, it forms silicon dioxide — an excellent electrical insulator that bonds perfectly to the silicon beneath it.

This meant you could build a transistor gate directly on the surface of the material, growing the insulating layer in place. The metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor (MOSFET) was born, and with it, the modern chip.

## The shrinking

For decades, engineers followed a pattern first described by Gordon Moore in 1965: the number of transistors on a chip doubled roughly every two years, as manufacturing processes grew more precise.

This required solving an extraordinary succession of problems — how to etch features smaller than the wavelength of light used to print them, how to prevent quantum tunneling when gate insulators become only a few atoms thick, how to keep billions of transistors from melting the chip beneath them.

Each solution opened the next problem. That is more or less the history of semiconductor engineering.

## Where we are now

The latest chips from TSMC and Intel have transistor gates measured in a few nanometers — though the naming conventions have become somewhat aspirational; a "3nm" process does not mean the gate is literally 3 nanometers wide. The actual geometries are proprietary and complex.

What is clear is that the classical approach to shrinking is reaching physical limits. The response has been to go vertical (stacking chips), wide (many cores), and specialized (chips designed for specific workloads). The transistor era is not over — but it is changing shape.
