I came to Borges late, and sideways — through a footnote in an essay about set theory. The essay mentioned a short story in which a library contains every possible book. I found the story. I read it at two in the morning. I did not sleep well afterward.

The story is called "The Library of Babel." It describes an infinite library whose hexagonal rooms contain every possible combination of letters — and therefore every book that has ever been written, every book that could ever be written, and an overwhelming majority of books that are pure nonsense, indistinguishable from the others without reading them.

## The mathematics underneath

Borges is often described as a writer who played games with ideas. This is true but undersells him. The games are precise. The Library of Babel is not a whimsical fantasy — it is a careful exploration of what infinity actually entails.

If the library contains every possible book, it contains every true book and every false one. A book correctly predicting the future sits somewhere among the shelves, surrounded by billions of books that differ from it by one letter. The library contains the cure for every disease, every proof of every theorem, and also every possible refutation of those proofs.

The horror Borges builds is not supernatural. It is logical. Total information, perfectly preserved, is indistinguishable from total noise.

## The self-referential trap

What struck me most on first reading was the ending, which I will not spoil except to say that it folds back on itself in a way that makes you feel, for a moment, that the story itself might be inside the library — which would mean that everything you know about the library was written there before Borges was born, which would mean...

This is the Borges move. He sets a logical machine in motion and lets you watch it consume itself.

## What to read next

If you have not read Borges, start with *Labyrinths*, the standard English anthology. After "The Library of Babel," read "The Garden of Forking Paths" and "Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius." Then put the book down for a week and think.
